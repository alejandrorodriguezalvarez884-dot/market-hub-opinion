At 8:30 this morning in Washington, the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes consumer prices for September. The forecasts gathered by Kiplinger point to a loud headline: prices up 0.6% in the month and 3.6% on the year, from 3.4% in August. Core prices, which leave out food and energy, are expected to rise 0.2% in the month and 2.5% on the year.

I think the headline will tell us little we do not already know. The useful part of today's report is the slow one.

## Why the headline is the easy part

The August report already showed where the heat is. Energy prices were 16.3% higher than a year earlier. The gasoline index rose 3.9% in August alone and made up over a third of that month's increase in all prices. Food, by contrast, was up 2.7% on the year, and the index without food and energy rose 2.4%, a little less than July's 2.5%.

A forecast of 0.6% for September says the same story went on. UBS economists, quoted by Kiplinger, put the monthly headline at 0.56% and core at 0.19%, which would take the yearly core rate down slightly, to 2.41% from 2.45%. If something close to that comes out, the headline will look alarming and will mostly be a report on oil.

That does not make it unimportant. Households pay for gasoline whether or not economists strip it out, and fuel finds its way into other prices: Kiplinger's own economist counts airfares among the things that pushed August up. But a headline driven by the pump says more about the world oil market than about anything the Federal Reserve can steer from Washington.

## The part that moves slowly

The figure I will look at first is services excluding energy: rent, insurance, medical care, a haircut, a meal out. These prices are set mostly by wages and contracts, so they move slowly, and once they speed up they tend to stay up. In the twelve months to August they rose 3.0%, according to the BLS. As Glenmede's strategists point out in Kiplinger's roundup, that is above a pace consistent with the Fed's 2% goal. It is the reason a quiet core number is not the same as a solved problem.

Shelter, the largest piece of it, rose 0.3% in August after 0.1% in July, and 3.0% on the year. A second firm month would say August was not a blip. A soft one would say the opposite.

## Why it matters this month

This is the last consumer price report before the Fed meets on October 27 and 28. In September the Fed raised its target range by a quarter point, to 3.75%-4%. Its statement said "inflation remains elevated" and that the move would support "a timelier return" to its 2% goal.

Last week [we argued](/opinion/article/?slug=the-fed-is-raising-rates-where-the-inflation-is-not) that the hike lands on people who are not the source of the inflation: the pressure is in oil, while the people who find money tight are home buyers and small firms. Today's report is the first new evidence on that view. If the rise is again mostly energy, the case that the Fed is pushing against the wrong thing gets stronger. If services pick up, the Fed's case does.

Markets were pricing about an 80% chance that the Fed leaves rates unchanged in October, according to a Capital.com analyst quoted by Kiplinger. That is what makes the print matter: prices react to the gap between the number and what was expected, not to the number alone. A 3.6% headline that everyone saw coming is old news a minute after it is out.

## What would change my mind

If core comes in at 0.3% or more again, with services doing the lifting, the "it is just energy" reading weakens, and so does the argument that September's hike was aimed at the wrong place. If core is 0.2% or lower and services cool, the opposite holds. Either way, the headline will get the attention, and the slow part will carry the information.
