On Tuesday Chevron said it will hand over to Hess Midstream its ownership interests in that company, its position as general partner, and its crude oil midstream assets in the DJ Basin. In return it gets $200 million in cash and a new set of contracts, which it expects to cut its Bakken unit midstream costs by approximately 50%. It also expects to recognize, at closing, a one-time after-tax loss of approximately $3 to $4 billion.

A footnote to that last figure runs to eight words: "Expected to be treated as a special item." I think the footnote deserves more attention than the deal.

## A customer that owned its supplier

Hess Midstream gathers, processes and ships the oil, gas and water that come out of the Bakken. It arrived at Chevron with Hess Corporation: its quarterly report names Chevron as its sponsor from July 18, 2025.

The same report shows how one-sided the relationship is. In the first half of 2026 Hess Midstream had revenues of $789.1 million, and $752.9 million of that was "affiliate services". That is 95 percent billed to Chevron and its affiliates. The agreements behind those fees include dedications of substantially all of Chevron's Bakken production, "minimum volume commitments" and "inflation escalators", and the main ones run through December 31, 2033. At June 30 Chevron's retained interest in the partnership was about 37.7 percent.

So Chevron was paying fees with inflation built in, under contracts with years left to run, to a company it partly owned. Part of what it paid came back as its share of the profits. The rest did not. Tuesday's deal ends the arrangement. Chevron stops being an owner and becomes only a customer, at about half the unit cost.

## Why it shows up as a loss

Chevron explains the loss in one clause. It arises "given that the company is unable to recognize future Bakken midstream cost savings as an asset."

In other words: what Chevron gives up is on its books, and what it gets is not allowed on them. A stake and some pipelines leave the balance sheet. A cheaper contract arrives, and accounting rules do not let a company write down "we will pay less from now on" as something it owns. Assets out, nothing visible in, and the difference is a loss.

I have no quarrel with that. It is the rule working as designed. But look at what the loss is in plain terms. It is the price of the cheaper contract. Chevron is paying $3 to $4 billion today, in assets and not in cash, to have lower costs for years. That is an investment, of the same kind as a pipeline it might have built itself.

## The scoreboard problem

This is where "special item" matters. Chevron's release describes its adjusted earnings as earnings without "significant non-operational items", a list that ends with "other special items", and says the measure helps in "comparing the underlying performance of its business across periods."

Follow the deal through that measure. The price is taken out, once. The saving stays in, every quarter, as lower operating costs. An investment whose cost never reaches the scoreboard and whose return always does will look good whatever was paid for it.

Had Chevron spent $3 billion of cash on equipment that halved the same costs, the equipment would be depreciated year after year, and adjusted earnings would carry that charge. Here the same bet carries none.

The return on capital claim needs the same care. Chevron expects the transaction to be "accretive to return on capital employed by 0.5% on an absolute basis." A ratio has two halves. Lower costs raise the return. But a write-off of several billion dollars, and taking Hess Midstream and its roughly $3.7 billion of debt off the consolidated accounts, also shrink the capital the return is measured against. The release does not say how much of the half point comes from each. This is my reading and not Chevron's: some of it is the denominator.

## The case for Chevron

The deal may well be a good one. Halving a cost on production the company says it expects to sustain is worth a great deal. A customer that is also the owner and the general partner of its supplier is an awkward thing, and this ends it. The other side was not a pushover either: the deal was approved by a committee of Hess Midstream's independent directors, with its own financial and legal advisors.

And nothing is hidden. The loss is in the second paragraph of the release, and the special-item treatment is in a footnote on the first page. That is more candour than many companies manage.

My complaint is with the scorekeeping, and it can be settled with three numbers the release does not give: the saving in dollars per year, how long the new contracts last, and what volumes Chevron has promised under them. With those, anyone can divide the price by the saving and see how many years it takes to pay back.

The transaction is expected to close by year-end 2026, and the final loss will be booked then. If those three numbers come with it, and the payback is short, I will say the price was low. Until then I am counting it as a price.
