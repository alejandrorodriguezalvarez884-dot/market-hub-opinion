On Wednesday the Federal Reserve published the minutes of its meeting of September 15 and 16, the one that raised the target range for the federal funds rate by a quarter point, to 3-3/4 to 4 percent. The vote was 12 to 0. Most readers went straight to one sentence: "most participants assessed that another increase in the target range for the federal funds rate would likely be appropriate by year end."

So the talk is about when. I read the document for a different question. Where does the Fed itself say the inflation comes from, and is that the place a higher rate lands? On the Fed's own account, I think the answer is no.

## Three causes, and wages are not one of them

The staff put total PCE inflation at 3.8 percent over the twelve months to August, and core inflation at 3.4 percent. Both were higher than a year earlier, and the staff said why. It attributed the rise "mostly to the effects of past tariff increases, higher energy and input costs stemming from geopolitical developments, and an increase in technology-related consumer goods prices associated with the AI buildout."

The policymakers said much the same. They pointed to crude oil and refined fuel, and to "surging AI-related investments".

Now look for the labor market in that list. It is not there. The employment cost index for private workers rose 3.3 percent in the year to June, and average hourly earnings 3.1 percent in the year to August, and the minutes note that both were below their level of a year before. Some participants said outright that "the labor market was not currently a source of inflationary pressures."

The jobs figures have not helped the hawkish reading since. The staff told the committee that payroll gains "picked up notably in August", and a majority of participants thought the labor market "had strengthened a bit recently". Sixteen days later the Bureau of Labor Statistics revised August down from 162,000 jobs to 133,000, revised July from a gain of 21,000 to a loss of 10,000, and reported 29,000 for September. Unemployment, which the minutes had at 4.1 percent, was 4.2.

## Where a quarter point is felt

The same document says, with some precision, who is feeling interest rates already. Financing conditions "remained generally accommodative for larger businesses and municipalities but were somewhat restrictive for residential mortgage borrowers and small businesses." Borrowing to buy a home "remained depressed". Meanwhile corporate bond issuance "remained robust, even beyond the recent notable activity of AI hyperscalers", the handful of companies building the data centers.

Put the two halves together. The inflation, as the Fed describes it, comes from an oil shock, from tariffs and from an investment boom paid for with heavy bond issuance, in markets that keep lending. The rate bites on mortgages and on small firms that borrow from a bank.

A quarter point does not bring down the price of crude. It is small change to a company selling long-dated bonds to build computing capacity it believes it must have. It is real money to a family pricing a mortgage. That is what I mean by raising rates where the inflation is not.

The minutes even describe the split among households. Stock market gains "had provided support to consumer spending, particularly among higher-income households", while low- and moderate-income households "faced strains, with higher energy prices weighing disproportionately on their real disposable income." It is the second group that has no stock gains to lean on when credit gets dearer.

## The best case for the hike

The Fed's answer is in the minutes too, and it is a serious one. It has one tool. Inflation has been above target for a long time, and some participants worried that "after more than five years of inflation above 2 percent" it could start to shape how wages and prices are set. Many called a higher path "prudent on risk-management grounds": insurance. Several judged that policy was "not restrictive or only mildly restrictive". And the staff expects growth to outpace the economy's potential through 2028.

I accept the logic of insurance. A central bank cannot refine oil, but it can stop an oil shock from becoming a habit. What I do not accept is the framing. Insurance has a premium, and these minutes show who pays it: the sectors that are already restricted, not the ones driving prices. And the risk being insured has not appeared. By the committee's own judgment, medium- and longer-term inflation expectations "remained at levels consistent with the Committee's 2 percent objective."

That may still be the right call. It should be argued as what it is, a decision about who carries the cost of an inflation that started elsewhere, and not as the cooling of an economy that is running hot across the board. The minutes do not describe that economy.

## What would change my mind

One line cuts against me. Several participants observed that price increases in core services excluding housing "remained elevated". Services are where domestic demand and wages show up. If that category keeps climbing while oil settles, the inflation is home-grown after all, and the hike is aimed correctly.

The other test is whether higher rates reach the boom itself: whether the borrowing that funds the AI buildout slows or gets dearer. The committee meets again on October 27 and 28. I will read those minutes for the same two things, before I read them for the next move.
