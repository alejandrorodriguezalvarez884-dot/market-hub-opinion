On October 30, FactSet put the S&P 500 at 18.7 times the earnings analysts expect over the next twelve months. That is below its five-year average of 19.8, below its ten-year average of 19.1, and well below the 22.0 the index started the year at. Measured against its own past, the stock market has got cheaper in 2026.

Turn that multiple upside down and it says something else. Paying 18.7 times earnings means that for every $100 put into the index, its companies are expected to earn about $5.35 over the next year. That is the earnings yield. The same day, the 10-year Treasury paid 4.94%, according to the St. Louis Fed. The difference, about four-tenths of a point, is roughly what a stock investor is offered for owning companies instead of lending to the government.

I think that gap, not the multiple, is the honest measure of how cheap stocks are today. And by that measure they are not cheap at all.

## How 2022 paid for a stronger rival

The comparison that matters is with 2022, the last time the bond turned into a serious rival. In mid-December 2021, FactSet put the forward multiple at 21.2, an earnings yield of about 4.7%. The 10-year paid 1.41%. Stocks offered more than three points over the bond.

Then yields climbed. On October 21, 2022, the 10-year paid 4.21%, its highest since 2008. Stocks made room for their new rival in the bluntest way: their prices fell. The multiple dropped to 15.6, an earnings yield of about 6.4%. Even with the bond at a fourteen-year high, stocks still offered about 2.2 points more. At the end of September 2022, with the multiple at 15.2, the gap was close to 2.8 points.

That year hurt. On FRED's closes, the S&P 500 went from 4,766 at the end of 2021 to 3,840 at the end of 2022, a fall of 19.4%. But the fall bought something. Whoever bought stocks in the autumn of 2022 was offered two to three points a year above the bond for the risk.

## How 2026 paid for it

This year the rival grew again. The 10-year ended 2025 at 4.18% and on October 5 reached 5.31%, its highest since May 2002. This time stocks did not fall to make room. The S&P 500 closed October 30 at 7,580, almost 11% above where it ended 2025.

The multiple fell anyway, from 22.0 to 18.7, so something else did the work: earnings. Analysts now expect S&P 500 earnings to grow 32.9% in 2026, according to FactSet, and FactSet's growth rate for the third quarter, which mixes the results already reported with estimates for the companies still to report, is 30.2%. By my arithmetic, a multiple 15% lower on a price 11% higher means the earnings the index is priced on rose by about 30% this year.

That is an extraordinary run, and it let stocks keep pace with a rising bond. But keeping pace is all they did. At the end of 2025 the earnings yield was about 4.55% and the 10-year 4.18%: a gap of about 0.37 points. On October 30 it was about 0.41. In between, on September 30, the multiple was 19.0 and the bond paid 5.29%, and the gap was gone: the Treasury paid a hair more than the earnings yield.

So the market of 2026 has not beaten the bond. It has drawn with it, at a margin about a fifth of the one investors were offered in October 2022. The usual line, that stocks are now cheaper than their average, is true and beside the point. The average was set in years when the bond paid much less.

## The strongest objection

A good objection is that the comparison mixes two different things. A Treasury pays a fixed number of dollars, and inflation eats them. Company earnings, at least in principle, grow with prices. The fairer rival, then, is the inflation-protected Treasury, whose yield is what the bond pays above inflation. On October 30 it paid 2.60%. Against it, stocks offer about 2.75 points, which looks comfortable.

But the comparison with 2022 does not improve. Against the same protected bond, stocks offered about 5.7 points in December 2021 and about 4.7 in October 2022. On this measure too, today's premium is roughly half of what it was then.

A second objection is that the gap has said nothing useful this year. It was just as thin in January, and the index has risen since. That is true, and it is why I do not read it as a timing signal. What the gap tells me is what holds the market up. In 2022, investors were paid to wait. In 2026, they are paid mostly by the earnings forecast coming true. A market priced like that is less exposed to the bond than it looks, and more exposed to the estimates.

Why yields rose this autumn is its own story, which this section has followed through [the inflation the bond market calls temporary](/opinion/article/?slug=bond-traders-call-this-inflation-temporary-because-they-expect-the-fed-to-fight-it) and [the rally in Treasuries in mid-October](/opinion/article/?slug=money-left-the-chips-for-the-dull-stocks-the-bond-rally-is-a-different-story). This piece is about what stocks offer against them.

## What would change my mind

The gap can widen in three ways: bond yields fall, prices fall, or earnings keep rising faster than prices. If the third happens, and FactSet's weekly reports show the forward estimates holding up while the index stands still, the cushion would rebuild itself without anyone having to pay for it, and I would be wrong to call it missing. If instead estimates start to slip while the 10-year stays near 5%, the market will have to find its cushion the way it did in 2022.
