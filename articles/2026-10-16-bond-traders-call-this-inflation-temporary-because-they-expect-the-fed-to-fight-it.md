Two days, two hot reports. On Wednesday the Bureau of Labor Statistics said consumer prices rose 0.6% in September and 3.7% over twelve months, up from 3.4% in August. On Thursday it said producer prices, what businesses charge each other, rose 1.3% in the month, the most since March 2022, and 6.3% over the year. Economists surveyed by Dow Jones and The Wall Street Journal had expected 0.6%, according to InvestmentNews.

The question everyone is asking is the one from 2021: is this temporary, or is it staying? I think the bond market has already answered, and its answer comes with a condition attached.

## The case that it passes

Most of the jump is fuel. Gasoline rose 9.1% in September and made up more than half of the rise in consumer prices. Energy is 20.4% more expensive than a year ago, and gasoline 34.2%. In the producer report, energy rose 10.8% in one month and gasoline 20.3%, and energy explained more than 80% of the rise in goods prices.

Fuel is the classic temporary shock. Prices at the pump can stop rising or fall back, and when they do the headline follows them. Food was flat in September. Goods outside food and energy rose just 0.7% in a year. The index without food and energy rose 2.6%: higher than August's 2.4%, but nowhere near the headline.

The bond market agrees. The gap between ordinary 10-year Treasuries and those indexed to inflation, the breakeven, is the inflation investors expect on average over the next ten years. On Thursday, after both reports, it was 2.32%, according to the St. Louis Fed. It was 2.36% on October 5. Two hot reports in two days did not move it up at all.

## The case that it stays

The slow part of the consumer report, the one I said to watch [on Wednesday morning](/opinion/article/?slug=todays-cpi-is-expected-to-jump-on-gasoline-read-the-part-that-moves-slowly), did move the wrong way. Services outside energy, which are set mostly by wages and contracts, rose 0.4% in the month and 3.1% in a year, from 3.0% in August. Shelter rose 0.4% and 3.2%. Core prices rose 0.3% in the month, against the 0.2% forecasters expected.

And energy is already leaking into things that are not energy. Airline fares are 22.4% higher than a year ago. In the producer report, transportation and warehousing services jumped 3.4% in a single month. Core producer prices, without food, energy and trade, are up 5.0% over twelve months. Producer prices are not consumer prices. But what a business pays this autumn is part of what it charges next spring.

This is how a temporary shock stays: not as gasoline, but as everything that needs gasoline to be made, moved or delivered.

## Where the two cases meet

So why is the bond market so calm? I think the answer is in a different market: fed funds futures.

In September the Fed raised its target range by a quarter point, to 3.75% to 4%. The minutes said most officials thought another increase would likely be appropriate by the end of the year. After Thursday's report, CME's FedWatch tool put the odds of a hike at the October 27-28 meeting at 33.8%, up from 29.3% before the release, according to InvestmentNews. For December it put 63.2% on one more quarter point and 30.6% on two. Add those up and, by my arithmetic, the market puts the chance of at least one more hike by the December meeting at about 94%. On Wednesday, at 12:20 pm in New York, the October odds had briefly touched 41.5%, by 24/7 Wall St.'s reading of the same tool.

Put the two markets together and the picture is consistent. Investors expect inflation to be temporary because they expect the Fed to raise rates until it is. The calm breakeven is not a judgement that the shock is harmless. It is a judgement that the Fed will treat it as if it were not.

That is a reasonable bet. It is also a bet with a counterparty. If the Fed holds in October and again in December, citing oil as something it cannot control, the breakeven would be the thing to watch. I argued [last week](/opinion/article/?slug=the-fed-is-raising-rates-where-the-inflation-is-not) that higher rates land on people who are not the source of this inflation. I still think so. But the bond market's calm shows the uncomfortable side of that view: the credibility of the hikes may be what is keeping long-run expectations anchored.

## What would change my mind

If services outside energy ease back toward 3.0% in the October report and the breakeven stays near 2.3%, "temporary" is winning on its own. If services keep climbing while the Fed hesitates and the breakeven rises with them, the market will be telling us the shock is turning into something that lasts. And if oil falls and takes the headline with it, we will learn how much of this was ever about the economy, and how much was about one price.
