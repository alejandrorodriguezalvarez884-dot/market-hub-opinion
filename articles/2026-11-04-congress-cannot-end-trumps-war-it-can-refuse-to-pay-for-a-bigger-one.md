The Democrats won Tuesday's midterms, and on the networks' projections they take both chambers of Congress in January: 54 Senate seats to 44 at 8 a.m. on Wednesday, with Alaska and Mississippi still open, and a House majority NBC puts at 258 seats or more. Donald Trump told the Wall Street Journal that the war with Iran "was a piece of it". In Tehran, the speaker of parliament wrote that Americans had said no to war.

So how much of his war has Trump lost? Less than the celebration in Tehran suggests, and more than his own shrug does. I think the new Congress gets a purse, not a veto. And on Wednesday morning, before the opening bell, prices said much the same.

## The arithmetic of a veto

A Democratic Congress can pass a binding war powers resolution directing the president to end the hostilities, as CNBC notes. Trump can veto it. Under Article I of the Constitution, a vetoed bill becomes law only if two thirds of each chamber pass it again: with everyone voting, 67 senators and 290 members of the House. Fifty-four is not 67. Unless thirteen Republican senators cross over, a war powers vote in 2027 will be a statement, as this summer's were.

The money is a different matter. The government is running on a stopgap that lasts until December 11, according to CSIS. For the 2027 fiscal year the White House asked for $1.15 trillion in regular defense money plus $350 billion through reconciliation, a route that needs only a simple majority. The budget resolutions Republicans wrote set aside $60 billion of it for defense. A separate $87.6 billion supplemental, sent on June 24 with $67.1 billion for the Pentagon and about a third of it driven by the war, is still waiting; Defense One reported in July that Democrats largely oppose it because it pays for a war Congress never authorized.

That is where the vote bites. CSIS wrote, a week before the election, that if Democrats won a chamber the lame-duck session would likely be Republicans' last chance at reconciliation, and that Democrats might prefer to wait for the new Congress to settle the year's numbers. From January, the majority that writes the Pentagon's spending bills will belong to the party that largely refused to pay for this war. Trump keeps the trigger. He loses the open checkbook.

## What the prices said

At 9:15 a.m. in New York, against Tuesday's close, Lockheed Martin was 3.8% lower, Huntington Ingalls 3.0%, RTX 2.9%, Northrop Grumman 2.8%, L3Harris 2.1% and General Dynamics 1.1%. Reuters put the reason plainly: the sector had leaned on a $1.5 trillion request meeting no resistance in a Republican Congress.

The telling part is what these shares did during the war itself. From the last close of February, just before it began, to Tuesday's close, Northrop fell 23%, L3Harris 23% and Huntington Ingalls 28%. Lockheed was 1% lower. The S&P 500 rose 10% over the same months, and Brent futures 43%. Eight months of fighting did not lift the contractors. They trade on appropriations, not on battles, and appropriations are exactly what changed hands on Tuesday.

Oil tells the same story from the other side. On Monday, after Iran said a Qatar-brokered plan could reopen Hormuz within two days and Trump twice answered "No" when asked if he would strike Iran after the midterms, Brent fell 7.6% to $104.77 and WTI 7.3% to $96.46. On Wednesday morning both were 2.7% lower, Brent at $100.77 and WTI at $93.46. The diplomacy moved the barrel nearly three times as much as the vote.

The quiet ones were quiet. The 10-year Treasury yield slipped from 4.95% to 4.92%. The dollar index fell 0.5%. Whatever the sweep does to the Pentagon's budget, the bond market did not treat it as a change in the country's borrowing.

## The case against

The strongest objection is that a purse is slow and a war is fast. CNBC cites reports that Washington has been preparing for possible renewed military action after the vote, and Iran's speaker warned that a betrayal of the vote would bring "a Nightmare". Nothing a Congress sworn in January does can stop a strike in November. The Pentagon is not penniless in the meantime either: it runs on the stopgap, and a war can be fought for a while on money already in the pipe. And Wednesday's oil drop came on a morning when S&P 500 futures were 1.2% higher and the VIX, Wall Street's gauge of expected swings, had fallen from 20.8 to 18.1. It may say more about relief than about Congress.

All true, and that is my point. The vote did not take the war out of Trump's hands; it took away the means to make it much bigger. Contractors priced that. Oil, sensibly, still looks to Doha and to the White House.

## What would change my mind

If Republicans use the lame duck to pass reconciliation with defense money near the $350 billion asked, the purse was not lost after all, and the contractors' fall was too quick. If Trump strikes again before January, the purse will have proved too slow to matter. The next dates are a reply from Washington to the Hormuz plan, and December 11, when the stopgap runs out and the outgoing Congress has to decide what it leaves to the incoming one.
