On paper, Monday was a good day. The Nasdaq 100 closed 1.17% higher, the S&P 500 0.41%, the Russell 2000 0.25%. The Dow slipped 0.07%. After a week in which chipmakers had lost more than a tenth of their value, a green close looked like a market finding its feet.

I read it the other way. Everything the Nasdaq 100 gained on Monday, it gained before the opening bell. From the open at 9:30 to the close at 4:00, the index fell 0.10%. The S&P 500 fell 0.16% over the same hours, and the chip index 1.36%. The close was green because the futures had done the buying overnight. The people who traded during the session spent most of it selling.

## The shape of the day

The numbers come from one-minute prices on Yahoo Finance, measured against Friday's close.

The Nasdaq 100 opened 1.27% higher, after futures had traded above Friday's settlement all night. It kept climbing until 11:37 am, when it stood 2.06% up. From there it slid for the rest of the session and closed at 1.17%, having given back more than two-fifths of its peak gain, and slightly below where it opened.

The chips told the same story more loudly. The PHLX Semiconductor index opened 3.01% higher and touched 3.71% at the same minute, 11:37. By 2:35 pm it was up only 1.40%, and it closed at 1.61%. More than half the morning gain was gone. The S&P 500 drew the same curve on a smaller scale: up 0.57% at the open, 0.94% at the top, 0.41% at the close.

Breadth was narrow. On the portal's sector measure, the average move of each sector's stocks, only three of eleven sectors ended higher: consumer cyclical (1.78%), technology (1.36%) and basic materials, which barely moved (0.06%). The other eight fell, utilities most of all (2.01%), followed by real estate (1.16%) and financials (0.83%). A day carried by two sectors, and by the hours before the session, is not a broad recovery.

## The oil leg did not hold either

On Monday morning I argued in this section that the bounce rested on cheaper oil, and that this was its weakest support. It did not even last the day. At 2:20 am Eastern on Monday, after President Trump suspended a list of sanctions on Russia on Friday evening, Brent was 2.2% below Friday's settlement, at $107.4 a barrel. It ended Monday at $109.55, just 0.3% lower. By 3:53 am on Tuesday it was at $110.70, up 1.0% on Monday. The 10-year Treasury yield rose too, from 5.10% to 5.14%.

And the morning after, the chip names gave back their Monday. At the 4 am premarket open on Tuesday, Nebius was 3.1% below Monday's close, CoreWeave 2.9%, Micron 2.2%, AMD 1.7% and Nvidia 0.4%.

## The case that it was a good day

The strongest objection is that the close is the number that counts, and the close was up. An investor who held the Nasdaq 100 from Friday to Monday made 1.17%. The VIX, the market's gauge of expected volatility, fell from 19.99 to 18.44. Fading from an intraday high is ordinary; most days have a high that the close does not keep. And after a week like the last one, a session that ends higher at all is not nothing.

I accept the arithmetic, and the VIX is a fair point against me. But the question after a selloff is not whether prices are higher than on Friday. It is whether buyers are willing to pay up while the market is open and the selling is visible. On Monday they paid up overnight, in the futures, and then gave ground for more than four hours once the session was under way. When a rebound has to be bought before the bell and is sold during it, I think it says more about the sellers than the buyers.

## What would change my mind

A session that closes near its high, not at its low, would. So would breadth: a day when most sectors rise, not three. The next real test comes on Wednesday, when Meta and Tesla report after the close. If the market then rallies during the hours it is open, Monday will look like a hesitant first step. If it rallies only overnight again and sells by the afternoon, Monday will look like what I think it was: a green number on top of a red day.
