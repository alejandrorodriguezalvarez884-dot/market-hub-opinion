On the afternoon of October 5, within an hour of one another, three large American companies filed a Form 8-K with the SEC. Vylor reported that it had become an independent company. Apollo reported an estimate of part of its third-quarter earnings. AbbVie reported a charge and lowered its forecast for the year.

Three stories with nothing in common, on one form. I want to make a small argument from that: the form is a better guide to what happened than the headline written about it, and it takes less time to read than most people assume.

## What the form is

A company files an annual report once a year and a quarterly report three times. Everything that cannot wait goes on a Form 8-K, which it must file within days of the event. The form is a list of numbered items, and the company marks which ones apply.

Those numbers are the part worth learning. They are a classification of the news made by the company's own lawyers, under rules, before anybody has decided how to tell the story.

Item 1.01 is a material agreement signed. Item 2.02 is results of operations and financial condition. Item 5.01 is a change in control of the company. Item 5.02 is a director or senior officer arriving or leaving. Item 7.01 is information the company is making public so that everybody has it at once. Item 8.01 is the catch-all for other events. Item 9.01 means documents are attached.

## Three filings, read by their numbers

Vylor's filing carried items 1.01, 3.03, 5.01, 5.03, 5.05, 8.01 and 9.01. Without reading further you know the shape of it: agreements signed, shareholders' rights modified, control of the company changed hands, its founding documents and its code of ethics amended. That is not a company reporting on its business. That is a company being constituted. The separation from Corteva is written in the item list.

Apollo's carried 2.02 and 7.01: something about results, disclosed so that everyone has it together. It turned out to be an estimate of alternative net investment income of about $375 million for the quarter, published four weeks before the full results.

AbbVie's carried 2.02 and 9.01: something about results, with a document attached. The document was a table showing $216 million of acquired research expense and a forecast for the year lowered by eleven cents a share.

## Same item, opposite news

Here is the part I find instructive. Apollo and AbbVie both filed under item 2.02. One was read as good news and the other as bad. Our own news desk tagged the first bullish and the second bearish.

Neither company had reported its results. Item 2.02 does not mean "the results are out". It means the company has said something material about them, and that can be the full quarter, an early estimate of one line, or a correction to a forecast. The number tells you the territory. Only the attachment tells you the direction.

And in both cases the attachment was short. Apollo's was a few paragraphs. AbbVie's was a table with a footnote. Reading either took less time than reading an article about it, and left nothing to interpretation that the company had not chosen to leave there.

## The objection, and my answer

The objection is obvious: nobody has time to read filings, which is what summaries are for. I write this on a site that publishes summaries, so I am not against them.

But a summary is a decision about what matters, made by someone else. Usually it is a good decision. Sometimes the word that carries a headline, "lowers", "reports", "completes", does more work than the facts under it can bear. AbbVie "lowered guidance" by the exact amount of a charge its guidance had never included. Apollo "reported" a figure that its own filing says no auditor has reviewed. Both headlines are accurate. Neither is the whole of what the form says.

My suggestion is modest. Learn the six or seven item numbers. When a filing matters to you, look at which items it carries, then open the attachment. Use the summary to find the filing and the filing to form the view.

The companies wrote these documents knowing they would be held to every word. That is a standard no headline is held to, including the ones on this site.
