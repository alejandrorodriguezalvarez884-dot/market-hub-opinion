ASML's third quarter, published this morning, is about as strong as an equipment maker's quarter gets. Sales of €11.7 billion, a gross margin of 57.5%, net income of €5.0 billion, and a fourth-quarter forecast of €14.2 to €15.2 billion. But the sentence that matters is not a number. The company now expects that its supply "as currently planned for both 2027 and 2028 will not fully meet the demand of our customers", and that some customers are interested in placing orders for 2029 and even 2030.

That is a remarkable thing for a toolmaker to say. I also think it explains, better than any analyst note, why Micron trades where it does.

## Two kinds of good news

Look at the prices at Tuesday's close, from Yahoo Finance on the portal. ASML traded at about 56 times its trailing earnings. Micron, now worth about $1.08 trillion, traded at 12.8 times the $74.33 a share it earned in its fiscal year. Sandisk traded at about 20 times.

Those trailing figures flatter the memory makers' multiples, because their earnings are still climbing fast. Micron guided its first fiscal quarter of 2027 to $61.5 billion of sales and $37.84 of earnings a share. Four quarters at that pace would be about $151, which puts the share price at roughly six times. Sandisk guided to $44 to $46 a share for its quarter; at that pace it trades at around eight times. That is my arithmetic on their guidance, not a forecast.

Micron earned $37.7 billion in its last quarter, on a gross margin of 86.8%. ASML earned €5.0 billion in its own. Yet each dollar of ASML's past earnings is priced at more than four times what a dollar of Micron's is. At first sight it looks absurd.

It stops looking absurd when you read the two releases side by side. ASML's good news is that its customers are ordering more machines than it can make. A large part of those machines go into memory fabs. Micron told investors on September 30 that it will spend more in fiscal 2027 than it had planned, about $25 billion in the first half alone, with most of the increase going to build cleanroom space that becomes available "in late calendar 2028 and beyond". The orders that make ASML's future look long are the same orders that make memory's future look crowded.

The market pays for how long a profit lasts, not for how big it is today. ASML's profit comes with a queue of customers and a service business, €3.3 billion of sales this quarter, that runs on the machines already installed. Memory's profit comes with a price, and everybody knows what happens to the price when the new fabs open.

## The memory that markets keep

They know because they saw it three years ago. In fiscal 2023 Micron sold $15.5 billion of memory and lost $5.8 billion. In fiscal 2026 it sold $133.2 billion. A business that can move that far in three years, in either direction, will not be given 50 times earnings, however good one quarter looks. Sandisk shows the same nerves at a smaller scale: on Tuesday its shares were about 37% below their 52-week high, after a year in which they rose more than twelvefold.

So the question is not when the market will reward another record. It has just watched Micron post its sixth quarterly revenue record in a row. The question is what would convince it that the records do not end the old way.

## What respect would take

I think the answer is already in Micron's filings, and it is the most interesting thing the company has said in years. It has signed 26 "strategic customer agreements": multi-year, take-or-pay contracts, some running into 2031. Customers have committed $32 billion, "the vast majority" in cash deposits. The contracts with a set pricing framework add up to about $150 billion of remaining obligations. Most of them have floor and ceiling prices, and Micron says that even at the floors it expects margins "meaningfully above any prior cycle peak margins". Sandisk has signed ten agreements of a similar kind, with prepayments and deposits it keeps out of its adjusted free cash flow.

That is memory trying to turn itself into something like ASML: a seller with a contracted queue rather than a seller with a spot price.

The strongest objection is that contracts signed in a shortage are tested in a glut. A buyer who agreed to a floor when memory was scarce will want to renegotiate when it is plentiful, and the seller will need that customer next year. The contracts also cover only part of the business. Micron says they are over 35% of its revenue through 2030, and three quarters of that has a pricing framework. By my arithmetic that leaves roughly three quarters of revenue still sold at whatever the market pays.

I take the objection seriously. It is why I do not read the low multiples as a mistake. They are a question.

## What would change my mind

The answer arrives on a known date. When the cleanrooms Micron is paying for start producing, from late 2028, and the industry's new supply meets demand, either the floors hold and the deposits stay where they are, or they do not. If they hold, I think memory will have earned a different kind of multiple, because it will have shown something it never could before: profits that survive a fall in prices.

Until then, ASML's warning that it cannot build enough machines is good news for ASML, and a reminder for everyone who buys them.
