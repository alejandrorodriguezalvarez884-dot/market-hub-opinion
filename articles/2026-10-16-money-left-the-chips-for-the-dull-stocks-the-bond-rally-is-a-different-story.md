Pick October 5 as the start. It was the last good day for chips: the VanEck semiconductor fund, SMH, closed at $633.90. Eight sessions later, on Thursday, it closed at $558.84, down 11.8%. Over the same stretch the consumer staples fund, XLP, rose 7.0%, the healthcare fund, XLV, 5.9%, and utilities 4.2%. The S&P 500 fund, SPY, barely moved: down 0.4%. The equal-weight version of the index, where Nvidia counts as much as a soup maker, rose 1.8%.

That is a rotation, and a clean one. The money did not leave the stock market. It left the part of it that had done the most and walked to the part that had done the least. On Thursday, by the portal's count, healthcare led the eleven sectors for the second day running, up 1.9%, while technology fell 0.7%.

What I am less sure of is the next step people are drawing from it: that the same money is heading into bonds.

## Software got one good day, not a trend

First, a correction to a story I have heard this week, that software is part of the escape. On Thursday it did look that way. Salesforce rose 4.6%, ServiceNow 6.1% and Adobe 4.6%, and the software fund, IGV, gained 1.8%. But over the eight sessions IGV is down 0.1%. It fell with the chips on Wednesday and came back on Thursday.

There is a case for software as the place to hide inside technology. Its costs are mostly people, not fabs. If the chip stocks are falling because investors worry about the debt being raised to build data centers, the companies that rent that capacity, rather than build it, are on the other side of that worry. I find that argument reasonable. One day of prices is not evidence for it yet.

## The bond rally, taken apart

Now the bonds. Their move is real. The 10-year Treasury yield, which hit 5.31% on October 5, closed at 5.09% on Thursday, according to Yahoo Finance. The long bond fund, TLT, rose 3.7%. Thursday alone was a big day: Investing.com called it the 10-year note's best day since early August 2025. It came the same morning producer prices rose 1.3% in a month.

A yield falling on a day of hot inflation needs explaining. The explanation people reach for is a flight to safety: money leaving stocks and buying government debt. Investing.com tied the move to worry about the heavy borrowing that funds AI infrastructure, and to wider spreads on corporate debt, including what it costs to insure technology companies' bonds against default.

Here is the detail I think matters. A nominal yield has two parts. One is what investors expect inflation to be. The other is the real return they demand on top of it. The Treasury sells bonds indexed to inflation, and the gap between the two kinds, published by the St. Louis Fed, splits the yield.

From October 5 to October 14, the 10-year yield fell 12 basis points, from 5.31% to 5.19%. Expected inflation, the 10-year breakeven, went from 2.36% to 2.35%. The real yield went from 2.95% to 2.84%. Almost the entire fall was in the real part. On Thursday the breakeven slipped to 2.32%.

So the bond market is not saying that inflation is going away. It still expects about 2.3% a year for a decade, as it did a week ago. What fell is the real return investors demand to lend to the government for ten years. That is what a flight to safety looks like. It is also what a market looks like when it starts to expect slower growth.

## Why I put a question mark on bonds

Three things keep me from calling this a rotation into bonds.

The first is the short end. The 3-month bill yield rose over the same days, from 4.22% to 4.28%. The 2-year yield hardly moved: 4.84% on October 5, 4.82% on October 14. Those are the maturities that track what the Fed will do next, and they are not rallying. Investors are buying the long end while expecting short rates to stay where they are or rise.

The second is scale. A 10-year yield of 5.09% is back where it was on September 23, and still above the 5.00% of a month ago. The rally has undone the last three weeks, not the climb.

The third is that the stocks that gained are bond-like themselves. Staples, healthcare and utilities pay steady dividends and are usually bought for the same reasons as Treasuries. When both rise together, it is often one decision, to own less risk, expressed twice.

## What would settle it

If the 10-year keeps falling while the breakeven stays near 2.3%, it is a bet on slower growth, and it would sit badly with a Fed that the futures market, by CME's FedWatch, expects to raise rates again by December. If the 2-year starts to fall too, the market is pricing a Fed that stops, and the bond case gets much stronger. And if the chip stocks recover and Treasuries give back their gains, this was a fright, not a rotation. One week tells us that money left the chips. It does not yet tell us where it means to stay.
