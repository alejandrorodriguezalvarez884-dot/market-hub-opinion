Alphabet's revenue grew 25% in the third quarter, to $127.6 billion, and Google Cloud's more than doubled. On October 29, the day after it reported, the stock fell 12.6%. Is that what a bubble looks like?

I do not think so. A bubble's defining habit is that it does not tell companies apart: it pays for the story, whoever tells it. Last week the market did little else but tell companies apart, and it did it harshly.

## Who was punished, and who was not

Look at the five reports side by side, with each share price measured from the close before its results to Tuesday's close.

Alphabet spent $51.4 billion on property and equipment in the quarter, more than twice the $24.0 billion of a year earlier, and its free cash flow was negative, minus $9.1 billion. Its revenue and earnings both fell short of the LSEG consensus, according to CNBC, the first such double miss in nearly four years (part of the profit gap was an $8.7 billion loss on equity holdings). The stock is down 13.8%.

Microsoft also more than doubled its spending on property and equipment, to $39.4 billion. But its revenue, up 19% to $92.3 billion, beat the consensus, and CNBC notes that it still produces positive free cash flow. The stock is down 1.9%.

Amazon is the case that matters most. It spent $77.3 billion in the quarter, against $35.1 billion a year earlier, and over the last twelve months its free cash flow was an outflow of $42.4 billion. If the market were simply punishing spending, Amazon would be the first to go. Instead AWS grew 39% to $46.0 billion, operating income rose to $28.1 billion from $17.4 billion, and the stock is 2.8% higher than before its report.

Two others got the harshest treatment of the week. Neither is one of the big spenders, but they show how little this market forgives. ServiceNow grew subscription revenue 21% and fell 27.0% in a day. Intel grew revenue 21%, beat on earnings per share, and then guided fourth-quarter revenue to $15 billion to $16 billion, against roughly $17 billion expected; it fell 28.6% in a day and is down 34.2% since.

The line here is not who spends. It is whether the spending already shows up in the numbers. And the severity is aimed, not general. FactSet reported on October 30 that the five "Magnificent 7" companies that had reported fell short of earnings estimates by 14.7% in aggregate, while the S&P 500 as a whole beat by 1.9%. Across the index, companies that beat were rewarded more than usual (an average gain of 1.6% around the release, against a five-year average of 0.9%) and those that missed were punished less than usual. The market is tough with the biggest AI spenders, not with everyone.

Compare that with the real thing. In the late 1990s, according to a study published in the Journal of Finance in 2001, companies that changed their name to a .com name saw abnormal returns of about 74% over the ten days around the announcement. The effect was much the same whatever a company's actual involvement with the internet. That is a market paying for a word.

On May 20, 1999, eToys went public at $20 a share and closed at $76 9/16. It was worth $7.78 billion, $2 billion more than Toys "R" Us, which was worth $5.65 billion. In its latest fiscal year eToys had $30 million in sales and losses of about $28.6 million; Toys "R" Us had sales of $11.2 billion.

The prices of the whole market said the same. On March 24, 2000, the S&P 500 traded at 24.4 times the earnings expected over the next twelve months. FactSet puts that ratio at 18.7 today, below its ten-year average of 19.1. Then came the reckoning: the Nasdaq Composite, which closed at 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000, fell to 1,114.11 by October 9, 2002, a loss of 78%. It did not set a new record until April 2015.

## The strongest objection

The best case against me is not about share prices. It is about the spending itself.

First, 18.7 is only modest against a decade that was expensive. In January 2020, FactSet noted that the same 18.7 was the highest forward multiple since 2002. And the "E" in that ratio is a forecast: analysts expect S&P 500 earnings to grow 32.9% this year. Part of what the rest of the market earns is what these few companies spend.

Second, the money is no longer coming only from profits. In the third quarter Alphabet bought back no shares, issued $12.4 billion of new ones and raised $28.7 billion in notes; its finance chief said capital spending will "increase significantly in 2027." A bubble can live in the investment even when share prices look sober. That is how overbuilding works, and I take it seriously.

Here is why it does not change my view. The objection describes a risk that the investment will not pay off. The market is the one asking that question, out loud, company by company, and charging those who cannot answer it yet. Buyers in a bubble do not ask. None of this means prices cannot fall: the Nasdaq Composite is already 4.4% below its close of October 28. A demanding market can still be a falling one. It is not a euphoric one.

## What would change my mind

If a company's shares jumped simply for announcing more AI spending, with no sign of a return. If big misses stopped mattering. If the forward multiple climbed back toward the mid-20s on forecasts alone. Any of those would look like 1999. Last week looked like an exam.
