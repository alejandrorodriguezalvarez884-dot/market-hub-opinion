Two releases came out of the Bureau of Economic Analysis on the same morning, the last day of September. One said the economy grew at an annual rate of 2.2 percent in the second quarter, with consumer spending first on the list of what drove it. The other said how consumers are paying for that. Read together, they are less cheerful than either is alone.

In August, personal income rose $66.6 billion, or 0.2 percent. Disposable income, what is left after taxes, rose $68.6 billion, or 0.3 percent. Personal consumption expenditures rose $190.8 billion, or 0.9 percent.

Spending grew almost three times as much as the income available to spend.

## Where the difference came from

There is no mystery about how that is possible. Money that is spent and was not earned in the month comes out of saving. The release gives personal saving of $990.2 billion and a saving rate of 4.1 percent of disposable income.

The arithmetic of the release itself says how much saving gave way. Disposable income rose $68.6 billion and personal outlays, which are spending plus interest payments and transfers, rose $190.7 billion. The gap is about $122 billion. That is my subtraction, not a figure the bureau prints in its summary, and like all the figures in these releases it is an annual rate. But the direction is not in doubt: in August, households closed the gap between what they earned and what they spent by saving less.

A saving rate of 4.1 percent leaves room to do that for a while. It does not leave room to do it for long.

## Growth with a thin base

Now put the second-quarter GDP release next to it. Growth of 2.2 percent, down from a revised 2.5 percent in the first quarter. The contributors the bureau names are consumer spending, investment and exports.

And put the September jobs report next to that. The economy added 29,000 jobs. Average hourly earnings rose 3.0 percent over the year.

So the largest engine of growth is the consumer; the consumer's income is growing slowly, because few jobs are being added and wages are rising at a moderate pace; and the consumer is spending faster than that income grows. Each of those facts is ordinary. Together they describe growth that depends on households being willing to keep saving less.

I think this is the weakest point in an economy that otherwise looks fine, and it gets less attention than it deserves because nothing about it is dramatic. Nobody writes a headline about a saving rate.

## The case against worrying

There are good objections, and they should be taken seriously.

One month is one month. Income and spending figures are noisy and they are revised, sometimes by a lot. A single August in which spending outran income proves nothing about September.

These are also figures in current dollars. Part of a 0.9 percent rise in spending is higher prices and not more goods, and the summary I am working from does not separate the two. If much of the increase was prices, the story is less about exuberant households and more about households running to stand still, which is a different problem but not a smaller one.

And households are not all alike. A national saving rate says nothing about who is saving and who is not.

Granted, all of it. What would change my mind is simple to state: income catching up. If the next releases show disposable income growing as fast as spending, August was noise. If they show hiring recovering from 29,000 a month, the income will come.

What I would not do is read 2.2 percent growth led by the consumer as a sign that the consumer is in good shape. On the evidence of the same morning's second release, the consumer is spending money it did not earn that month. That can go on. It cannot go on indefinitely, and the number that says when is the saving rate, not GDP.
