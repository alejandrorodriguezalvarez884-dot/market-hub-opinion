On October 6 Sysco closed a very large bond sale: $10.75 billion of senior notes, $3.9 billion of junior subordinated notes in dollars and €1.0 billion of junior subordinated notes in euros. The money is to pay part of the cash price of Jetro Restaurant Depot, which Sysco agreed to acquire on March 30.

Start with the size. The prospectus has a table of what the company owed on June 27: $13.5 billion. The same table, adjusted for this financing, shows $30.2 billion.

The part worth a closer look is the junior notes. The table carries the euro ones at $1.13 billion, so the two together come to roughly $5 billion. They are debt. They are also designed so that, to some of the people who judge Sysco's debt, they do not fully count as debt.

## What a junior subordinated note is

All four series fall due in 2056, thirty years out. They rank behind every senior note Sysco has, old and new. Their coupons are fixed for the first five to ten years and then reset every five years to a market rate plus a spread. On the Series A notes that is the five-year Treasury rate plus 2.280%, and the spread steps up by a quarter point in 2037 and to a full point in 2052.

Then comes the clause that makes them what they are. The issuers "may, at their option, defer payments of interest" on the notes "on one or more occasions for up to ten consecutive years". Skipping a coupon on a senior note is a default. Skipping it here is a right the company bought.

It is not a free pass. Deferred interest keeps accruing, with additional interest on top, and all of it has to be paid before current interest can start again.

## What the right costs

Sysco sold senior and junior paper on the same day with the same final year, so the price can be read off the page. The senior notes due 2056 pay 6.500%. The junior notes due 2056 pay 7.100%, 7.250% and 7.350%.

That is 0.60 to 0.85 points more a year. On the $3.9 billion of dollar notes it comes to about $28 million a year, by my arithmetic. The comparison is rough, since the junior coupons will reset and the senior one will not. But that is the order of magnitude of the premium.

## Why pay it

For the credit rating. A bond that lets the borrower stop paying for a decade without defaulting works, in a bad year, a little like a share. The prospectus speaks of the "equity credit" that rating agencies assign "to securities such as" these notes. It does not say how much credit Sysco expects. It does show how much the credit matters: if an agency changes its method so that the notes earn less of it, or earn it for less time, Sysco may redeem them.

So if these notes ever stop counting as partly equity, the company has kept the right to take them back. That tells you what they are for.

Meanwhile, for tax, the prospectus says the notes "should be treated as indebtedness", and adds that no ruling has been sought from the IRS. Debt to the tax collector. Something softer to the rating agencies.

I see nothing improper in it. Every term is printed in the prospectus. But I think a reader of Sysco's balance sheet should count these notes as what they are: thirty-year debt at about 7%.

## Equity-like for whom

Not for the shareholder. During a deferral, the prospectus says, Sysco may not, with some exceptions, "declare or pay any dividends or distributions on, or redeem, purchase, acquire" its own stock.

Follow the order of events in a bad year. The moment the junior coupon is skipped, the dividend and the buyback have to stop. The senior lenders are paid throughout. So the cushion that earns the equity credit is one the shareholders provide. A hybrid looks like equity to everyone above it and like debt to everyone below it. Shareholders are below it.

## The objection

What else was Sysco to do? More senior debt would lean harder on the rating. More new shares would dilute the owners for good, and the sellers of Jetro Restaurant Depot are already being paid partly in shares. A note in between, at a premium of under a point, is a reasonable tool. If Sysco never defers a coupon, it will simply have paid for insurance it did not use.

I accept all of that. My point is about how to read the bonds, not about whether they should have been sold.

## What to watch

First, whether the deal closes. If it has not by March 30, 2028, or a later date the parties agree, most of these notes must be redeemed. The exception is the senior notes due 2036.

Second, how leverage is quoted once it does. When someone gives Sysco's debt as a multiple of its earnings, ask whether the junior notes are in the figure whole.

Third, January 6, 2032, the first reset date of the Series A notes. Whether Sysco takes them back then or lets them reset will say how much it still needs the cushion.

What would change my view is debt falling quickly after closing. Then the ten-year right would have been cheap insurance, bought and never needed.
