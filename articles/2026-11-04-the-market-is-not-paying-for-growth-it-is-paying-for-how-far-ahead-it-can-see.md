At Tuesday's close, six of the best-known names of the AI trade (Nvidia, Micron, Sandisk, SK Hynix, Alphabet and Meta) traded at a median 9.6 times the earnings analysts expect from them over the next twelve months. Four of the best-known defensive stocks (Walmart, Coca-Cola, Costco and Procter & Gamble) traded at a median 33.4 times. Analysts expect the first group to grow earnings per share by 21.9% in its next fiscal year, and the second by 8.0%. Divide the multiple by the growth and you get the PEG ratio: 0.20 for the AI group, 4.08 for the defensives.

Read literally, the market pays twenty times more for each point of growth at a soft-drink maker than at a chip designer. I do not think the market is confused. I think it is answering a different question. The PEG asks how fast earnings grow next year. A share price answers how many years of earnings you can see. On that question the market is charging a great deal for calm.

## How the numbers are built

These companies close their fiscal years in different months: Nvidia and Walmart in January, Micron and Costco around the turn of August and September, Sandisk and P&G in midsummer, the rest in December. So for each I took Yahoo Finance's consensus earnings per share for the current and the next fiscal year and weighted them by the months left, so that every multiple covers the same next twelve months. Growth is next fiscal year's consensus against this year's. I use medians, so that SK Hynix at 2.8 times, or the 24% fall analysts expect in Alphabet's earnings next year, do not drag a whole group. SK Hynix trades in won and is compared only through ratios. Free cash flow is that of the last four reported quarters, divided by market value. These are my calculations on Yahoo's figures, not anyone's forecast.

## Three different reasons to see less far

Memory is the easy part. Micron, Sandisk and SK Hynix trade at 2.8 to 4.7 times because the market has watched memory earnings peak and collapse before; I argued in October that [this gap tends to close from the other side](/opinion/article/?slug=micron-is-not-waiting-for-the-market-to-catch-up-memory-multiples-close-from-the-other-side). Set them aside and the puzzle is still there. Nvidia at 14.5 times, Alphabet at 17.8 and Meta at 18.7 cost less than half of what Walmart (39.7) or Costco (44.0) cost.

For Nvidia, the horizon is its customers' budgets. In the first half of its fiscal 2027, three direct customers accounted for 16%, 15% and 13% of its revenue, according to its 10-Q: 44% from three buyers. Walmart's revenue comes from millions of shoppers who need groceries next year whatever happens. Nvidia's depends on what a handful of companies decide to spend, and they decide again every quarter.

For Alphabet and Meta, the issue is cash. Their earnings look cheap; their cash does not. Over the last four reported quarters Alphabet's free cash flow came to 0.6% of its market value and Meta's to 1.8%, against 3.5% at Coca-Cola and 4.1% at P&G. In the third quarter Alphabet's capital expenditure was $51.4 billion, more than twice the $24.0 billion of a year earlier, and its free cash flow was minus $9.1 billion. Meta spent $33.9 billion, against $18.8 billion, and its free cash flow was minus $1.2 billion. Alphabet's long-term debt went from $46.5 billion at the end of 2025 to $125.1 billion at the end of September. Its earnings are also unusually padded this year: in the first nine months of 2026 it booked $127.5 billion of other income, against $26.6 billion a year before.

So the market is not really pricing these two on earnings. It is pricing the cash left over after the building, and for now that is very little. I find that reasonable.

## The strongest objection, and a test

The best case against my reading is simple: forecasts made at a peak are the least reliable kind, and the AI multiples are low because the earnings in them are too high. That is fair, so test it. How far would earnings have to fall for each of these to cost what the median defensive costs? Nvidia's by 57%, Alphabet's by 47%, Meta's by 44%.

That has happened before, and not only in memory. Meta's earnings per share fell 38% in 2022, from $13.77 to $8.59. A repeat from here would leave Meta at about 30 times, between Coca-Cola (27.1) and Walmart (39.7). In other words, today's prices treat something close to a 2022 as the base case for the platforms, and something close to 2023 as the base case for memory. Not as the bad case. As the expected one.

Now look at the calm side. At 33.4 times, the median defensive earns about 3.0% of its price, while the 10-year Treasury yields 4.95%. The stock market as a whole [barely pays more than that bond](/opinion/article/?slug=the-sp-500-got-cheaper-this-year-and-still-pays-almost-nothing-over-a-treasury); these four pay less. Coca-Cola's shares are up 39.5% in a year and posted their highest close of that year on November 2, while analysts expect its earnings per share to grow 11.4% this year and 5.6% next. Paying that multiple for that growth is a bet too: that the growth runs, unbroken, for a very long time. It is a bet that does not feel like one.

My view is that the market is right to see less far in AI than in groceries, and that it now charges too much for the difference. Calm has become the expensive thing.

## What would change my mind

Nvidia reports on November 17. If its customer list gets even more concentrated, the short horizon earns its discount. If Alphabet's and Meta's capital spending keeps outrunning their operating cash flow, the number that matters for them is the cash yield, not the PEG, and on that measure they are not cheap at all. And if Coca-Cola delivers its 5.6% and keeps its multiple, then visibility is worth even more than I think, and the premium I call steep is simply the going rate.
