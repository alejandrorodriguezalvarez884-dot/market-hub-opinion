On Friday the Federal Reserve published its Survey of Consumer Finances, the picture of American families' balance sheets that it takes every three years. Read one line of it and households look sounder than at any time in the five surveys the report tabulates. Read another and they look more stretched than in any of them. I think the second line is the one that matters, and that the first gets quoted more because it flatters.

## The flattering ratio

Among families with debt, the median leverage ratio, which is what a family owes divided by what it owns, was 26.9 percent in 2025. It was 29.2 percent in 2022 and 38.6 percent in 2013. The report calls this a "steady decline".

It did not happen because families paid debt down. The report says median and mean debt were unchanged from 2022. It happened because what they own went up. Median net worth rose 2 percent after inflation, to $215,900, and the mean rose 7 percent, to $1.24 million. For families that own a home, the median net housing value went from $218,900 to $230,000. For families that own stock, the median holding grew 36 percent, to $77,400.

## The other ratio

Now measure the same debt against income. The median family with debt spent 15.4 percent of its income on debt payments, up from 13.4 percent in 2022. The share of families spending more than 40 percent of income on them rose from 6.5 to 8.6 percent, above the 8.2 percent of 2013.

Then comes the line I had to read twice. In 2025, 19.6 percent of families said they had been late on a loan payment in the preceding year. In 2022 it was 12.2 percent. Those late by two months or more went from 4.9 to 8.2 percent. Both are the highest figures in the report's table, which starts in 2013.

The report's summary puts the two views in one sentence: "Borrowers look less financially fragile when their debts are scaled to their assets rather than to their income."

## Why I side with income

A loan payment is made out of a paycheck. Nobody settles a credit card bill with a slice of the kitchen. The report says the same in drier words: "families may have money tied in illiquid assets that may not easily be drawn down to finance debts."

There is a second reason. A ratio that improves because house and share prices rose improves most for those who own houses and shares, and they are not everybody. Stock market participation fell from 58 to 56 percent, and the report says the fall was "driven by families outside the top 10 percent of the income distribution." Families that typically have less wealth, such as the young, renters and those without a high school diploma, saw their median wealth decline.

Meanwhile the money itself costs more. The report says the rise in payment burdens "may be related to the concurrent increase in interest rates for mortgages and consumer loans." Among families with debt secured by their home, the median amount rose from $170,200 to $183,600. Among the 45 percent or so who carry a credit card balance, the median rose by $150 to $3,100 and the mean by $1,300 to $8,000.

## The case against my reading

It is a good case. The hard measures of distress barely moved. In 2025, 1.4 percent of families had declared bankruptcy in the past five years, which the report calls "largely unchanged". Foreclosure proceedings touched 0.7 percent, against 0.5 percent. Payday loans were taken by 2.6 percent, against 2.1 percent. Median income rose 7 percent after inflation, to $82,200. Fewer families have student debt, and those who do owe less.

And "late" is a wide net. The survey asks families with debt whether they were behind on any loan payment in the past year. One missed instalment counts the same as a household going under.

So this is not a picture of collapse, and I do not read it as one. I read it as a picture of thin margins. Lateness is what shows first, long before the bankruptcy column moves, if it ever does. And a rise of more than seven points in three years, over a period when the same report shows homes and shares gaining value, is too large for me to wave away as noise.

## What I would watch

The report says how many families are late, not on what. The Fed publishes the survey's detailed data alongside it. If the lateness turns out to sit in one kind of loan, the story narrows and I would narrow my view with it. If it is spread across mortgages, cards and cars, then the two ratios are measuring two different things. The leverage ratio is telling us about asset prices. The late-payment line is telling us about families.

The next survey is three years away. Until then, when someone says household balance sheets are in good shape, it is worth asking which side of the balance sheet they looked at, and whether they looked at the paycheck at all.
