US stock futures were higher before dawn in New York on Monday. At 2:20 am Eastern, Nasdaq 100 futures were up 1.4% on Friday's settlement, S&P 500 futures 0.8% and Dow futures 0.4%, by Yahoo Finance's data. Brent crude was down 2.2%, at $107.4 a barrel.

The morning's reports tied the two together. Investors were going back into technology after last week's rout, and oil was easing because President Trump, on Friday evening, suspended a long list of sanctions on Russia and waived proposed tariffs on countries that buy its oil, days before they were due to take effect. Lawmakers of both parties criticised the decision.

I think the bounce is standing on the wrong leg. Cheaper oil is good news, but it is not what broke the AI trade last week, and a waiver signed on a Friday evening, with lawmakers of both parties against it, is the easiest piece of the story to undo. What broke the trade was a question about cash: who pays for the data centers, and out of what. That question has a date with an answer, and it is not today.

## What last week priced

From the close on October 9 to the close on October 16, the Nasdaq Composite fell 4.5% and the Nasdaq 100 5.5%. The S&P 500 lost 1.9%. The PHLX Semiconductor index fell 11.4%, and CoreWeave, which rents out AI computing power and borrows heavily to build it, lost 25.2%. On Friday alone, technology was the worst sector on the portal, down 3.0%, while energy led, up 2.5%.

Look at what did not move against stocks. The 10-year Treasury yield ended the week at 5.10%, below the 5.24% where it began. If the selloff had been about the general price of money, falling long-term yields would have cushioned it. Yields fell and technology fell anyway. The trouble was specific. As this section wrote on Wednesday, it started in a CoreWeave bond that briefly traded at a yield above 15%, as SiliconANGLE reported, and spread to every company that borrows to build computing capacity.

Monday's futures have won back part of that. Nasdaq 100 futures fell from 31,108.75 to 29,391.50 over the week; at 2:20 am they were at about 29,800. That is roughly a quarter of the drop.

## The cash is in Meta's statement

Companies like CoreWeave rent their capacity to the biggest buyers of computing. The biggest buyers pay for their own build-out from what their businesses earn, and Meta's last report shows how thin that cushion has become.

In the second quarter, Meta generated $31.86 billion of cash from its operations and spent $31.08 billion on capital expenditures, counting principal payments on finance leases. Free cash flow was $784 million. A year earlier it was $8.55 billion. It paid $1.35 billion of dividends and bought back no shares, against $10.17 billion of buybacks a year earlier. And it raised $24.91 billion of new long-term debt in the quarter.

And the plan is to spend more. Meta expects 2026 capital expenditures of $130 billion to $145 billion. On the same measure it spent about $50.9 billion in the first half. To land in that range it has to spend $79 billion to $94 billion in the second half, roughly $40 billion to $47 billion a quarter. That is more than the $31.86 billion its operations brought in during the second quarter. Unless that cash flow grows fast, the gap comes out of the $90.26 billion of cash and securities Meta held at the end of June, or from more borrowing. Its long-term debt was already $83.66 billion.

This is the question the market asked of CoreWeave last week, asked one level up. A cheaper barrel of oil does not answer it. Meta's third-quarter results, due after the close on Wednesday, October 21, will answer it for one more quarter.

## The case for the bounce

The strongest objection is that I am mixing up two very different companies. Meta is not CoreWeave. It took in $60.8 billion of revenue in the second quarter, up 28% on a year earlier. It holds $90 billion in cash and securities and can slow its spending whenever it chooses. A company like that funding a build-out from its balance sheet is not a credit problem, and selling everything with AI in its story because one heavily indebted company's bonds wobbled was overdone. On this reading, a rebound is just an overreaction correcting itself.

I agree with most of that. Meta can pay. But Meta's spending is other companies' revenue: the chipmakers', the neoclouds', the builders of data centers. When the free cash flow of one of the biggest buyers falls to almost nothing, the whole chain depends on that buyer's willingness to keep spending ahead of the returns. That willingness, not Meta's solvency, is what investors started to doubt last week. A Monday morning of cheaper oil does not tell us anything new about it.

## What would change my mind

If Meta's operating cash flow on Wednesday shows it is catching up with its spending, or if the company can show that the spending is already earning its keep in revenue, then the rebound has a firmer floor than the price of oil, and last week will look like a scare. If Meta raises its spending range again while its cash flow stands still, then Monday's bounce rests on the oil price alone, and the oil price this morning rests on an order signed on Friday. Tesla reports the same day, and its spending will be read the same way.
