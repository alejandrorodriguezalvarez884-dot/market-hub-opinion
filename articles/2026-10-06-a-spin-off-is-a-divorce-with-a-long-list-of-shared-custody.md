At 12:03 in the morning of October 1, New York time, Corteva's seed business stopped being part of Corteva. It became Vylor, a company with its own shares, its own board and its own ticker on the New York Stock Exchange. Every holder of Corteva stock on September 24 received Vylor shares in proportion, and Corteva kept none.

That is the clean version, and it is true. The filing Vylor made four days later tells a second story alongside it. In the last days before the split, the two companies signed six agreements with each other. A spin-off, it turns out, is less a clean break than a divorce in which the lawyers have written down who keeps what, who pays which bill, and who still has to pick up the phone.

## What the six agreements are for

The filing names them. Each exists because something could not be cut in two on the day.

A **Separation and Distribution Agreement** is the master document: which assets and which liabilities go to which company, and what each owes the other if a claim from the past turns up later.

A **Tax Matters Agreement** deals with the fact that a spin-off of this kind is normally built to be tax-free for shareholders, and stays that way only if both companies behave in certain ways afterwards. It usually restricts what the new company may do with itself for a time.

An **Employee Matters Agreement** divides people, pensions and pay plans.

A **Transition Services Agreement** is the admission that the new company cannot yet run by itself. For a period the old parent keeps providing things such as systems, payroll or accounting, for a fee.

An **Intellectual Property Matters Agreement** settles who owns which patents and names, and who may keep using the other's.

And a **Global Master Seed Treatment Supply Agreement** is the one specific to this business: the two companies will keep trading with each other, one supplying the other, as customer and supplier rather than as divisions.

I have described what agreements of each kind generally do. The terms of these particular ones are in the documents attached to the filing, and that is where anyone who wants to know the details of this separation should look.

## Independent, with an asterisk

None of this is unusual, and none of it is a criticism. It would be more worrying if the agreements did not exist. A business that has lived inside a larger one for years shares its plumbing with it, and plumbing is not rerouted at midnight.

But it does qualify the word "independent". On its first day of trading, Vylor depended on its former parent for services it does not yet provide for itself, was bound by tax rules written to protect the parent's shareholders, and had its former parent as a counterparty in a supply contract. Corteva, for its part, has a new supplier or customer whose interests are no longer its own.

The filing also records what a company does on its first day alive: a new certificate of incorporation and bylaws, corporate governance guidelines, a code of conduct for directors, another for officers and employees, a code of ethics for senior financial officers. A board that existed on paper is now accountable to shareholders who never chose to own this company. They were handed it.

## What I would read first

The argument for a spin-off is always focus: two businesses with different needs, each better run and better understood apart. Sometimes that is true. Sometimes what is created is two head offices where there was one. Nobody can tell which on the first day, and I distrust anyone who claims to.

What can be told early comes from the unglamorous agreements, not from the press release. How long the transition services run says how ready the new company really was. The price terms of the supply agreement say which of the two got the better side of the relationship it could not end. And the first annual reports will show what each company costs to run alone, which is the one number the focus argument has to beat.

A spin-off is announced as an ending. The paperwork says it is the start of a relationship with new terms, and those terms are where its success or failure will show first.
