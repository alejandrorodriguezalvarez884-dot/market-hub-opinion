The September jobs report had a number built to be ignored. Employers added 29,000 jobs. The unemployment rate was 4.2 percent, inside the band of 4.1 to 4.3 percent it has not left since March. The Bureau of Labor Statistics used its favourite phrase for months like this one: employment "changed little".

I think that phrase is hiding the story. The market is not weak in the way we usually mean, with layoffs rising and people queuing for benefits. It is still. And a labor market that has stopped moving is a different animal from one that is merely slow.

## Nobody in, nobody out

Look at the turnover figures the bureau published three days earlier. In August there were 7.1 million job openings, 5.2 million hires and 5.1 million separations. Of those separations, 3.1 million were people quitting and 1.6 million were layoffs and discharges. The layoff rate was 1.0 percent. The quits rate was 1.9 percent.

Put the two reports side by side and the picture is consistent. Firms are not firing: a layoff rate of 1.0 percent is not what a downturn looks like. But they are not hiring either, and workers know it, which is why so few of them quit. Hires and separations cancel each other almost exactly, and what is left over is a payroll gain of a few tens of thousands.

That is why the unemployment rate does not move. It is not that the economy is creating enough jobs to absorb everyone who wants one. It is that few people are losing a job and few are finding one. The rate is flat because the doors on both sides of the room are nearly shut.

## The revisions say more than the headline

The headline number is the least informative figure in the release. The revisions are the most.

July was first reported as a gain of 21,000 jobs. It is now a loss of 10,000. August was reported as a gain of 162,000 and is now 133,000. Together that is 60,000 jobs that were in last month's report and are not in this one. The average gain over the previous twelve months was 45,000 a month, so two months of revisions erased more than a normal month.

A month below zero has already happened. It simply arrived late and quietly, as a revision, with no headline of its own.

The detail by industry points the same way. Health care, which has carried hiring for a long time, added 17,000 jobs against an average of 33,000 over the prior twelve months. Financial activities lost 7,000, and insurance carriers are down 90,000 since their peak in May 2025. Construction added 11,000 and manufacturing 9,000, which is welcome and small.

## Why stillness is not safety

The comfortable reading is that a frozen market is a stable one. Wages are still growing, 3.0 percent over the year to $37.81 an hour. The workweek held at 34.4 hours. Participation held at 61.8 percent. Nothing is breaking.

I do not find that comforting, for one reason. A market with strong hiring can take a rise in layoffs: people who lose a job find another. A market with hiring this low cannot. If the layoff rate went from 1.0 percent to something only slightly higher, there would be no flow of new jobs to meet it, and the unemployment rate that has sat in a band for six months would leave it quickly.

The strongest objection is that this has been true for months and nothing has happened. That is fair. Stillness can last, and firms that are reluctant to hire are often just as reluctant to let people go. A low-hire, low-fire economy is not doomed to become a low-hire, high-fire one.

But it does change what deserves attention. The payroll headline will keep saying "changed little" until the day it does not. The figures I would watch instead are the two that would move first: the layoffs and discharges rate in the turnover report, and the revisions to the months already published. If layoffs stay near 1.0 percent, the stillness holds. If the revisions keep coming in negative, the stillness was already something else, and we were told a month late.
