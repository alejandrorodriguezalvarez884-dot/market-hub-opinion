On Wednesday the companies that rent out AI computing power fell together. CoreWeave closed at $65.78, down 15.5%. Nebius closed at $186.01, down 10.1%. DigitalOcean fell 14.8%, and Oracle, which is not a neocloud but has borrowed heavily to build data centers, lost 8.8%. Technology was the worst sector of the day on the portal, down about 3%.

The spark was not a sales miss. It was a bond. A $1.25 billion issue of CoreWeave notes due 2032, with a coupon of 9.625%, briefly traded at a yield above 15%, as SiliconANGLE reported. A hotter inflation print the same morning, 0.6% in September with gasoline up 9.1%, made money look more expensive for everyone.

My view is that the market priced a credit problem that belongs mostly to CoreWeave, and charged it to the whole group. For Nebius in particular, I do not think the fall says much about the business. Its last filings describe a company that grows faster and pays far less to fund itself.

## Two ways to pay for a data center

Start with CoreWeave's own second-quarter release. It sold $2.6 billion of services, up about 112% on a year earlier, and had a backlog of about $104 billion. Demand is not its problem. Its problem is what sits under the revenue: about $35 billion of debt on the balance sheet, against $5.5 billion of cash, and $640 million of net interest in one quarter. That is roughly a quarter of its revenue, and five times its adjusted operating income of $128 million. At Wednesday's close the market valued the whole company at about $30 billion, less than it owes.

Nebius has funded itself differently, and its documents show it. Its letter to shareholders for the second quarter says roughly 70% of the deals it closed included customer prepayments, covering 50% to 60% of the capital spending tied to them. It expects more than $9 billion of prepayments this year. That is money from customers, not from lenders.

When it has borrowed, it has borrowed cheaply. In July it raised about $775 million secured on deployed GPUs and a contract with an investment-grade customer, at SOFR plus 2.50%. In August it sold $5.0 billion of convertible notes: $3.0 billion due 2030 at 0.50% and $2.0 billion due 2034 at 4.50%, convertible at about $313 and $325 a share. It also sold 12.7 million shares through its at-the-market program at an average of $223.60, raising about $2.8 billion. It ended June with $8 billion of cash, before the August notes.

And it is growing much faster than the group. Second-quarter revenue was $582 million, up 454%. Annualised run-rate revenue reached $3.0 billion in June, up 56% in three months, and the company kept its target of $7 billion to $9 billion by the end of the year. Its AI cloud business earned an adjusted EBITDA margin of about 50%.

I think a company that pays a 0.50% coupon and collects prepayments is not exposed to a 15% yield on someone else's bond the way the selloff implied. The money Nebius raised at $224 a share is in the bank. A lower share price on Wednesday does not take it back.

## The strongest case against me

The objection is real, and it has four parts.

First, a convertible is still debt. The 0.50% coupon is cheap because buyers were promised a share of the upside. At Wednesday's close the stock was about 40% below the 2030 notes' conversion price. If it stays there, those notes are repaid in cash, and a convertible that will not convert is just a bond.

Second, Nebius is not done raising. Its capital spending guidance for 2026 is $20 billion to $25 billion, against revenue of $3 billion to $3.4 billion. It plans to deploy more than a gigawatt a year from 2027. Prepayments of $9 billion cover only part of that, and the rest will come from markets that, on Wednesday, showed how fast they can change their minds.

Third, some of the good numbers are the company's estimates. Its payback period of one year and ten months counts capacity not yet built. It also lengthened the useful life of its servers from four years to five, which lowers depreciation and helps margins.

Fourth, every share sold through the program dilutes the people who already own the company.

I accept all four. None of them is new, and none of them is what happened on Wednesday. Coverage of the day also cited a Gartner warning that 80% of neoclouds could disappear by 2030. I could not find it among Gartner's published releases. What Gartner did publish in June is a forecast that neoclouds will take 20% of a $267 billion AI cloud market by 2030. A market that size has room for a shake-out. I think the pressure will fall first on the companies that borrowed at close to 10% to build, not on the ones whose customers pay up front.

## What would change my mind

Three things, all of which Nebius will have to show. The next debt it raises, which its letter says may be asset-backed or corporate, should cost something close to what it paid in July; a spread far above SOFR plus 2.50% would mean the market's fear has reached its own funding. The share of deals with prepayments should stay near 70%. And run-rate revenue should land in the $7 billion to $9 billion range by December.

If those hold, Wednesday will look like a day when the market read the price of one company's bonds and applied it to another company's balance sheet.
